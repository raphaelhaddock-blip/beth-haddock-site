## Historical Context: The Crash and Its Causes

### The Roaring Twenties and Speculation Fever

**What was happening:**
- The 1920s saw unprecedented industrial expansion
- Profits were invested in speculation, especially stocks
- Stock prices rose "by virtue of their desirability" rather than fundamentals
- An atmosphere of "rampant and unrestrained speculation" developed

**The Margin Buying Problem:**
- Ordinary people could buy stocks with as little as 10% down payment
- Borrowed the rest from stockbrokers
- Intended to sell quickly at higher prices before payment came due
- "There was little actual money supporting the stocks' values"
- Some investors took bank loans just to cover the initial margin

**Key insight:** The system worked as long as prices kept rising. When they stopped, the entire edifice collapsed.

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - Wall Street Crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_crash_of_1929), [Federal Reserve History](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1929)

### The Crash of 1929

**Timeline:**
- **August 1929:** Federal Reserve raised discount rate from 5% to 6%
- **September-October 1929:** Margin requirements tightened, prices began falling
- **October 24, 1929 ("Black Thursday"):** 12.9 million shares traded in panic
- **October 29, 1929 ("Black Tuesday"):** 16.4 million shares traded

**The mechanism of collapse:**
1. Utility stock prices fell
2. Margin buyers forced to sell (margin calls)
3. Forced selling drove prices lower
4. More margin calls triggered
5. Panic selling of all stocks

**Aftermath:**
- Dow Jones dropped 35% in the year after May 1969
- 160 NYSE member firms disappeared (half to merger)
- October 1970: Goodbody and Company (5th largest brokerage) failed
- Beginning of the Great Depression (lasting until 1939)

**Sources:** [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/what-caused-the-stock-market-crash-of-1929), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/stock-market-crash-of-1929)

## The Pecora Investigation (1932-1934)

### The Investigation That Changed Everything

**Background:**
- Senate Banking Committee investigation into causes of the 1929 crash
- Started March 4, 1932 but made little progress
- Bank executives denied requests for records
- Witnesses easily evaded questions

**The Turning Point:**
In early 1933, the committee hired Ferdinand Pecora, a former New York deputy district attorney, as chief counsel. Everything changed.

### What Pecora Exposed

**Wall Street Abuses Uncovered:**
- Unethical tax practices
- Insiders given investment advantages not available to public
- Investors misled about substandard securities
- Short selling manipulation
- "Pooling" techniques to manipulate stock prices
- Underwriting and sale of shaky securities
- Interest-free bank loans to insiders and favored clients

**High-Profile Takedowns:**
- **J.P. Morgan:** Forced to admit under oath he paid no income taxes in 1930 and 1931
- **Morgan Partners:** Controlled 89 corporations worth $19 billion
- **Preferential Stock Deals:** Morgan gave special deals to politicians from both parties
- **National City Bank (now Citibank):** President forced to resign after exposé
- **Richard Whitney:** NYSE president interrogated

**The "Banksters":**
Pecora's hearings coined the term "banksters" - finance "gangsters" who had imperiled the economy.

### The Impact

**Prior to 1933:** Federal government did not concern itself with Wall Street

**After Pecora:**
- Wall Street subjected to public accounting for first time in U.S. history
- Shattered reputations, resignations, firings, jail sentences
- Educated American public about financial institutions
- Forever changed relationship between Wall Street and Washington

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - Pecora Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pecora_Commission), [U.S. Senate Historical Office](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/pecora.htm), [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-man-who-busted-the-banksters-932416/)

## The Securities Act of 1933 ("Truth in Securities")

### Creation and Context

**Enacted:** May 27, 1933

**Drafted by:** Benjamin V. Cohen, Thomas Corcoran, and James M. Landis

**Signed by:** President Franklin D. Roosevelt

**Part of:** The New Deal

**Purpose:** Congress's initial effort to regulate securities - the first major federal securities law

### The Two Core Objectives

1. **Require disclosure:** Investors receive financial and other significant information concerning securities being offered for public sale

2. **Prohibit fraud:** Prohibit deceit, misrepresentations, and other fraud in the sale of securities

### The Disclosure Philosophy

**The key insight:** The 1933 Act is based on a philosophy of disclosure.

> "The goal of the law is to require issuers to fully disclose all material information that a reasonable shareholder would need in order to make up his or her mind about the potential investment."

**Critical distinction from state laws:**
> "Unlike state blue sky laws, which impose merit reviews, the '33 Act embraces a disclosure philosophy, meaning that in theory, **it is not illegal to sell a bad investment, as long as all the facts are accurately disclosed.**"

### Registration Requirements

**What companies must file:**
- Registration statement including prospectus
- Copious information about the security and company
- Audited financial statements
- Business description
- Risk factors
- Management team and compensation

**Section 5:** All issuers must register non-exempt securities with the SEC

**Section 11:** Issuers strictly liable for registration statements containing:
- Untrue statements of material fact
- Omissions of material facts required to make statements not misleading

### Exemptions

**Not all offerings must be registered:**
- Private offerings to limited number of persons/institutions
- Small offerings (SEC can exempt based on "small amount" or "limited character")
- Regulation D offerings (Rule 506)

**Policy rationale:** Foster capital formation by lowering cost of offering securities to public

### Anti-Fraud Provisions

**Section 17:** Regardless of whether securities must be registered, the 1933 Act makes it illegal to commit fraud in the offer or sale of securities

**Investor remedies:** Investors who purchase securities and suffer losses have recovery rights if they can prove incomplete or inaccurate disclosure

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - Securities Act of 1933](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities_Act_of_1933), [Cornell Law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/securities_act_of_1933), [SEC.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/registration-under-securities-act-1933)

## The Securities Exchange Act of 1934

### Creation of the SEC

**Enacted:** June 6, 1934

**Key Innovation:** Created the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)

**First Chairman:** Joseph P. Kennedy (yes, JFK's father)

**First Commissioners:** Included Ferdinand Pecora himself

### Scope and Authority

**The 1934 Act regulates secondary trading** (trading between persons often unrelated to the issuer) - contrasted with the 1933 Act which regulates primary offerings.

**SEC Powers:**
- Register, regulate, and oversee brokerage firms
- Regulate transfer agents and clearing agencies
- Oversee self-regulatory organizations (SROs like FINRA, NYSE)
- Identify and prohibit certain market conduct
- Disciplinary powers over regulated entities

### Ongoing Disclosure Requirements

**Where the 1933 Act requires one-time disclosure, the 1934 Act extends this to continuous disclosure:**

- **Form 10-K:** Annual report with audited financials
- **Form 10-Q:** Quarterly report
- **Form 8-K:** Material events (within 4 business days)
  - Change of CEO
  - Change of auditing firm
  - Destruction of significant assets
  - Other material developments

**Threshold:** Companies with $10M+ in assets and 500+ shareholders must file

### Registration Requirements

The Act requires registration for:
- Exchanges
- Brokers and dealers
- Transfer agents
- Clearing agencies

**All filings available through EDGAR** (Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval)

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - Securities Exchange Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities_Exchange_Act_of_1934), [Cornell Law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/securities_exchange_act_of_1934)

## SEC v. W.J. Howey Co. (1946) - The Howey Test

### The Case Facts

**The Parties:**
- W.J. Howey Company owned citrus groves in Lake County, Florida
- Planted ~500 acres annually
- Sold half to public "to help us finance additional development"
- Howey-in-the-Hills Service Company cultivated, harvested, and marketed crops

**The Scheme:**
1. Buyer purchases land (often through resort hotel marketing)
2. Buyer signs service contract leasing land back to service company
3. Service company tends land, harvests, pools, and markets produce
4. Profits remitted to investor

**Key Details:**
- Service contract gave Howey "full and complete" possession of the land
- Buyers had no right of entry or right to the produce
- Most buyers were NOT farmers - they were "business and professional people inexperienced in agriculture"
- 85% of buyers signed service agreements
- Howey "promised significant profits" in sales pitch

### The Legal Question

Was this an "investment contract" (and therefore a "security") under Section 2(a)(1) of the Securities Act of 1933?

### The Ruling

**Supreme Court (May 27, 1946):** Reversed lower courts. This IS a security.

Justice Frank Murphy wrote:

> "The test of whether there is an 'investment contract' under the Securities Act is whether the scheme involves an investment of money in a common enterprise with profits to come solely from the efforts of others."

### The Four-Part Howey Test

An "investment contract" exists when there is:

1. **An investment of money**
2. **In a common enterprise** (fortunes tied to others)
3. **With a reasonable expectation of profits**
4. **Derived primarily from the efforts of others** (promoter or third party)

### Key Principles Established

**Form over substance:**
> "Form [is] disregarded for substance and the emphasis [is] on economic reality."

**Broad investor protection:**
> "The policy of the Securities Act of affording broad protection to investors is not to be thwarted by unrealistic and irrelevant formulae."

**Why this matters for crypto:**
The Howey test is "often used to evaluate unconventional financial arrangements, such as cryptocurrencies."

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - SEC v. Howey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEC_v._W._J._Howey_Co.), [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/328/293/), [Cornell Law - Howey Test](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/howey_test), [SEC Framework for Digital Assets](https://www.sec.gov/files/dlt-framework.pdf)

## Disclosure vs. Merit Regulation

### Federal Approach: Disclosure-Based

**Philosophy:** The government doesn't decide if an investment is "good" or "bad." It ensures investors have the information to decide for themselves.

**Implications:**
- It is legal to sell a bad investment if fully disclosed
- Government is not in the business of evaluating investment merit
- Focus is on preventing fraud and ensuring transparency

### State Approach: Merit-Based (Blue Sky Laws)

**Origin of name:** Laws meant to protect investors from schemes that have "no more basis than so many feet of 'blue sky.'"

**Philosophy:** State regulators CAN deny registration to securities they deem "unfair or unjust" - even if all disclosures are made.

**How it works:**
- Over 40 states apply merit review to state-registered offerings
- Regulators evaluate whether offering is "fair, just, and equitable"
- Can block offerings that meet disclosure requirements but seem unfair

**Examples (Texas standards):**
- Promoter's participation exceeds 10% of total equity? → May be denied
- Options/warrants exceed 10% of public offering? → May be denied
- Offering price significantly exceeds insider price? → May be denied
- Unreasonable amounts of "cheap stock"? → May be denied

### Federal Preemption

**NSMIA (1996):** National Securities Markets Improvement Act

**"Covered securities"** are largely exempt from state registration:
- Securities listed on national exchanges
- Certain Regulation D offerings (Rule 506)

**States retain:**
- Intermediary licensing authority
- Antifraud enforcement authority

**Sources:** [Cornell Law - Blue Sky](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/blue_sky_law), [Wikipedia - Blue Sky Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_sky_law)

## SEC Enforcement Evolution

### The SEC Mission (Three Parts)

1. **Protect investors**
2. **Maintain fair, orderly, and efficient markets**
3. **Facilitate capital formation**

### Historical Evolution

**1934-1970s:** Focus on enforcing 1933 and 1934 Acts - transparency and fraud prevention

**Post-2008 Financial Crisis:**
- Enforcement Division resources increased 50%+
- Expanded to cybersecurity, market manipulation, emerging technologies

**2022:** Commission recovered three times its budget in enforcement actions (most in history)

### Enforcement Chairs: Clayton vs. Gensler

| Metric | Jay Clayton (2017-2021) | Gary Gensler (2021-2024) |
|--------|------------------------|-------------------------|
| Crypto enforcement actions | 70 | 125 |
| Crypto actions resolved | 50 | 98 |
| Total penalties | ~$1.52B | ~$6.05B |
| Fraud allegations | 54% of actions | 66% of actions |
| Unregistered offering allegations | 71% of actions | 63% of actions |

**Common ground (both chairs):**
> "Without prejudging any one token, most crypto tokens are investment contracts under the Howey Test."

**Key difference:**
Clayton acknowledged need for guidance that "doesn't leave the ecosystem high and dry." Gensler pursued "regulation by enforcement."

**Industry complaint (both eras):**
The use of enforcement actions in lieu of explicit rulemaking or guidance that companies can use as a "bright line test."

**Sources:** [CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2021/03/02/state-of-crypto-how-sec-chair-gary-gensler-could-differ-from-predecessor-jay-clayton), [Poliscio Analytics](https://poliscio.com/huge-turnaround-in-secs-crypto-enforcement/)

## Collision Points with Crypto

### The Howey Test Problem

**Traditional Securities:**
- Clear issuer (company selling stock)
- Clear promoter (management team)
- Clear common enterprise (the corporation)
- Clear profits from efforts of others (management creates value)

**Crypto Tokens:**
- Often no clear issuer (open-source projects)
- Decentralized development (who is the "promoter"?)
- Common enterprise? (Network vs. corporation)
- Efforts of others? (Users participate in network)

**The fundamental tension:** Howey was designed for 1946 citrus groves with clear hierarchies. Crypto has distributed networks with diffuse responsibility.

### The "Sufficiently Decentralized" Question

**SEC's Hinman Speech (2018):**
Suggested that if a network becomes "sufficiently decentralized," it may no longer satisfy Howey.

**The problem:**
- No clear test for "sufficient decentralization"
- Projects may start centralized, become decentralized
- At what point does the security become a non-security?

### The Disclosure Problem

**Traditional IPO:**
- Single company files registration
- Management provides disclosures
- Auditors verify financials
- Ongoing reporting requirements

**Crypto Token:**
- Who files the registration?
- Decentralized projects have no management to disclose
- What financials? (Open-source code)
- Who is responsible for ongoing disclosure?

**The framework mismatch:** Securities law assumes a corporate issuer. Many crypto projects don't have one.

### Registration Impossibility

**SEC Position:** Most tokens are securities and must register.

**Industry Response:** There's no registration path for most tokens.

**The catch-22:**
- SEC says "come in and register"
- But existing forms (S-1, etc.) were designed for corporations
- No clear pathway for decentralized protocols
- Result: Enforcement without compliance option

## Why This Matters

The entire regulatory framework for securities was created in response to:
1. A speculative bubble built on margin debt
2. Fraud and manipulation by insiders
3. Lack of disclosure to ordinary investors
4. No accountability for those who caused harm

**The core philosophy:** Protect investors through mandatory disclosure and fraud prohibition.

**The crypto question:** How do you apply a 90-year-old framework designed for corporations to decentralized networks with no central authority?

This tension is the heart of the regulatory collision.
