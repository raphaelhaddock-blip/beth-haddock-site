## Historical Context: Why Banking Is So Heavily Regulated

### The Problem: Fractional Reserve and Bank Runs

**How banks work:**
- Banks don't keep cash equal to total deposits
- They lend out deposits to earn interest
- Only a small fraction is kept as reserves
- Works fine... until everyone wants their money at once

**Bank run mechanics:**
1. Depositors fear bank will fail
2. They rush to withdraw funds
3. Bank can't meet all withdrawals (funds are lent out)
4. Fear spreads, more people withdraw
5. Bank actually fails (self-fulfilling prophecy)

### The Great Depression Banking Crisis

**Pre-Depression (1921-1929):**
- ~5,700 bank failures (mostly rural)

**During Depression (1929-1933):**
- Nearly 10,000 bank failures
- More than one-third of ALL U.S. banks
- Panic spread from state to state

**The Caldwell Collapse (November 1930):**
- Large financial conglomerate in Nashville collapsed
- Triggered runs in Tennessee, Kentucky, Arkansas, North Carolina
- Marked the onset of the first banking crisis of the Depression

### The FDIC Solution (1933)

**Banking Act of 1933:**
- Signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt
- Created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)
- Most controversial element of the law

**Initial structure:**
- Temporary Fund effective January 1, 1934
- Basic coverage: $2,500
- Initial capital: ~$289 million (from Treasury and Federal Reserve Banks)

**Banking Act of 1935:**
- Made FDIC permanent

**The record:**
> "Since the start of FDIC insurance on January 1, 1934, no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds as a result of a failure."

### Evolution of Coverage

| Year | Coverage Limit |
|------|---------------|
| 1934 | $2,500 |
| 1950 | $10,000 |
| 1969 | $20,000 |
| 2024 | $250,000 |

### 2023 Bank Failures - Recent Test

- Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, First Republic Bank
- 2nd, 3rd, and 4th largest failures in FDIC history
- Government determined "systemic risk"
- Protected ALL depositors, including uninsured

**Sources:** [FDIC History](https://www.fdic.gov/about/history/deposit-insurance/1930-1939.html), [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-does-deposit-insurance-work/), [Wikipedia - FDIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Deposit_Insurance_Corporation)

## The Bank Secrecy Act (1970)

### Creation and Purpose

**Enacted:** October 26, 1970 (signed by President Nixon)

**Official name:** Currency and Foreign Transactions Reporting Act

**Why it was created:**
- Response to "increasing reports of people bringing bags full of currency of doubtful origin into banks"
- Criminals using "secret foreign bank accounts" for money laundering
- U.S. banks couldn't detect or prevent this activity
- One of the world's first pieces of dedicated anti-money laundering (AML) legislation

### Core Requirements

**What financial institutions must do:**
1. Keep records of cash purchases of negotiable instruments
2. File reports if daily aggregate exceeds $10,000
3. Report suspicious activity (potential money laundering, tax evasion, criminal activity)

**Purpose:**
> "Provide law enforcement authorities with greater evidence of wrongdoing by collecting information for investigations and proceedings to punish criminals and deter future illegal activity."

### Evolution of the BSA

**1970:** Original Bank Secrecy Act passed

**1986:** Anti-Drug Abuse Act / Money Laundering Control Act
- Made money laundering a criminal activity (first time!)
- Previously, just reporting violations

**1990:** FinCEN established by Treasury Order

**1992:** Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) introduced

**1994:** FinCEN gains regulatory responsibilities (administers BSA)

**2001:** USA PATRIOT Act
- Biggest expansion of BSA
- FinCEN becomes bureau within Treasury
- Extended requirements to more institutions
- Anti-terrorism financing provisions

**Fun fact:** Within two years of passage, BSA was invoked in Watergate investigation (campaign financing violations).

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - BSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_Secrecy_Act), [FinCEN BSA](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-and-regulations/bank-secrecy-act), [FinCEN History](https://www.fincen.gov/history-anti-money-laundering-laws)

## FinCEN: The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network

### Creation and Role

**Established:** April 25, 1990 (Treasury Order 105-08)

**Part of:** U.S. Department of the Treasury

**Original purpose:** Analyze BSA information to prevent financial crimes

**2001 expansion:** USA PATRIOT Act made it a full Treasury bureau

### Mission

> "To safeguard the financial system from illicit activity, counter money laundering and the financing of terrorism, and promote national security through strategic use of financial authorities and the collection, analysis, and dissemination of financial intelligence."

**Motto:** "Follow the money."

### Key Functions

1. **Implement and enforce BSA** and AML laws
2. **Central repository** for financial transaction data
3. **Analyze data** to detect suspicious activities
4. **Collaborate** with law enforcement and regulators
5. **Serve as U.S. Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU)** - one of 147 globally in the Egmont Group

### FinCEN vs. State Regulators

**Key distinction:**
- **FinCEN:** Sees itself as money laundering preventer
- **State regulators:** See themselves as consumer protectors

This difference shapes how each approaches crypto and fintech regulation.

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - FinCEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Crimes_Enforcement_Network), [FinCEN Mission](https://www.fincen.gov/about-fincen/mission)

## The U.S. Dual Banking System

### How Bank Charters Work

**The fundamental split:**
- **Federal charters:** Issued by Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
- **State charters:** Issued by individual states

### National Banks (Federal Charter)

**Chartered by:** OCC (under National Bank Act of 1863/1864)

**Characteristics:**
- Names include "National," "N.A.," or "NT&SA"
- Automatically Federal Reserve members
- Examined by OCC only (single federal regulator)
- Typically larger banks

**Types:**
- Commercial banks
- Limited purpose trust banks
- Special purpose banks (e.g., credit card banks)

### State-Chartered Banks

**Two sub-types:**

**State Member Banks:**
- Join Federal Reserve System (optional for state banks)
- Regulated by state + Federal Reserve

**State Non-Member Banks:**
- Don't join Federal Reserve
- Regulated by state + FDIC
- FDIC is primary federal regulator
- ~5,000 banks supervised by FDIC

### Key Differences

| Factor | National Bank | State Bank |
|--------|--------------|------------|
| Charter | OCC | State banking dept |
| Fed Membership | Required | Optional |
| Primary Federal Regulator | OCC | FDIC (if non-member) or Fed (if member) |
| Assessment Costs | Higher | ~40% lower (varies) |
| Deposit Insurance | Required | Required |

### Special Purpose Charters

**National Trust Companies:**
- OCC chartered
- Generally uninsured
- Restricted to fiduciary activities, custody
- Cannot take demand deposits or make loans

**Fintech Charter (2016):**
- OCC announced special purpose charter for fintech firms
- Faced legal challenges
- Limited adoption

**Wyoming SPDI:**
- Special Purpose Depository Institution
- State charter for crypto companies
- Can accept deposits without FDIC insurance

**Sources:** [OCC Charters](https://www.occ.gov/topics/charters-and-licensing/index-charters-licensing.html), [Federal Reserve FAQ](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/banking_12779.htm)

## Money Transmission Licensing

### Origins and History

**Why these laws exist:**
> "The origins of money transmission licensing were haphazard state-by-state responses to predatory corner shops that would fleece vulnerable populations when selling a money order."

**Original targets:**
- Western Union
- MoneyGram
- Money orders
- Traveler's checks
- Remittance providers

**The statutes are archaic:**
> "Many were conceived and drafted prior to the invention of the floppy disk, and were never intended to address anything more exotic than a wire transfer. The men who crafted these laws never considered that a computer could slip into a backpack, let alone store millions of dollars in convertible value."

### The Consumer Protection Philosophy

**Unlike banking regulators (who worry about systemic risk), state money transmission regulators worry about consumers:**

> "A chief objective of money transmission regulation is to guarantee the continued solvency of the transmitter."

**The key requirement:**
- Money transmitters must hold customer funds 1:1
- Unlike banks, NOT supposed to engage in risk-taking
- No fractional reserve lending
- Pure custody of customer funds

### The State-by-State Patchwork

**Current status:**
- 49 states regulate money transmitters (except Montana)
- Each state has different requirements
- Licensing requirements vary widely:
  - Surety bonds: $25,000 to $1,000,000+
  - Net worth requirements
  - Permissible investments
  - Examination schedules

**The compliance nightmare:**
> "The variation among 50 states has resulted in differing standards, which has created an unnecessarily complex compliance environment for companies operating in multiple states."

### Modern Harmonization Efforts

**Money Transmission Modernization Act (MTMA):**
- Created by state regulators + industry
- Approved by Conference of State Bank Supervisors (CSBS)
- Single set of nationwide standards
- 31 states have enacted (full or partial) as of 2025

**What MTMA standardizes:**
- Net worth (capital) requirements
- Surety bond requirements
- Permissible investments (liquidity)

**Sources:** [Coin Center - Money Transmission](https://www.coincenter.org/education/policy-and-regulation/money-transmission/), [Modern Treasury - MTL](https://www.moderntreasury.com/journal/how-do-money-transmission-laws-work), [CSBS MTMA](https://www.csbs.org/csbs-money-transmission-modernization-act-mtma)

## Crypto and Money Transmission

### FinCEN 2013 Guidance (FIN-2013-G001)

**Significance:** First U.S. regulatory agency to issue guidance on virtual currency

**The framework - three categories:**

1. **User:** Obtains virtual currency to purchase goods/services
   - **NOT** a money services business (MSB)
   - No registration required

2. **Exchanger:** Business exchanging virtual currency for real currency or other virtual currency
   - **IS** a money transmitter
   - Must register with FinCEN
   - Must comply with BSA

3. **Administrator:** Issues and can redeem virtual currency
   - **IS** a money transmitter
   - Must register and comply

**The key principle:**
> "The definition of a money transmitter does not differentiate between real currencies and convertible virtual currencies. Accepting and transmitting anything of value that substitutes for currency makes a person a money transmitter."

### FinCEN 2019 Guidance (FIN-2019-G001)

**Published:** May 9, 2019

**Purpose:** Most significant CVC guidance since 2013 - consolidate and clarify

**Key statement from FinCEN:**
> "The money transmitter definition we published in 2011 and the guidance we issued in 2013 clarifying how that definition applies to transactions involving virtual currency have proven to be exceptionally durable. Despite dynamic waves of new financial technologies, products, and services, our original concepts continue to hold true."

**Wallet classification:**
Depends on four factors:
1. Who owns the value?
2. Where is the value stored?
3. Does owner interact directly with payment system?
4. Does intermediary have total independent control over value?

**Hosted wallets** (Coinbase-style) = money transmitters
**Self-custody wallets** (user holds keys) = NOT money transmitters

**Mining exemption:**
> "To the extent that a user mines Bitcoin and uses the Bitcoin solely for the user's own purposes and not for the benefit of another, the user is not an MSB."

**Software development exemption:**
> "The production and distribution of software, in and of itself, does not constitute acceptance and transmission of value."

**Sources:** [FinCEN 2013 Guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-regulations/guidance/application-fincens-regulations-persons-administering), [FinCEN 2019 Guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/new-fincen-guidance-affirms-its-longstanding-regulatory-framework-virtual)

## Collision Points with Crypto

### The State-by-State Problem

**Traditional money transmitters (Western Union):**
- Brick and mortar in specific states
- Clear physical presence
- Made sense to regulate state-by-state

**Crypto companies:**
- Internet-native, no physical presence
- Serve customers in all 50 states from day one
- Must get 49+ licenses to operate legally
- Cost: millions of dollars, years of effort

**The impossibility:**
> "State and local regulation was more sensible back then because these brick and mortar businesses were typically based in and only had customers in that state."

Modern crypto companies face a framework designed for 1950s corner money order shops.

### The Definition Gap

**What FinCEN says:**
User → not MSB
Exchanger → MSB
Administrator → MSB

**What's unclear:**
- DeFi protocols (no administrator)
- Smart contracts (no human intermediary)
- DAOs (decentralized governance)
- Self-custody wallets (user holds keys)

### The Consumer Protection Question

**Traditional money transmission:**
- Company holds your money
- If company fails, you lose money
- 1:1 reserve requirement protects you

**Crypto self-custody:**
- YOU hold your money
- No company to fail
- No reserves needed (you hold the asset)
- What exactly is state regulators protecting?

### The Federal Gap

Unlike securities law (SEC) or commodities law (CFTC), there is NO federal money transmission license.

**Result:**
- Crypto companies must navigate 49 state regimes
- Each with different requirements
- Each with different interpretations of what crypto activity triggers licensing
- Enormous compliance burden

**Proposed solutions:**
- Federal fintech charter (OCC - faced legal challenges)
- Money Transmission Modernization Act (state-level harmonization)
- Congressional action (various bills proposed)

## Why This Matters

The money transmission and banking regulatory framework was built for:
1. Protecting depositors from bank runs (FDIC)
2. Preventing money laundering (BSA/FinCEN)
3. Ensuring money transmitters can pay customers back (state licensing)

**Crypto challenges each assumption:**
- Self-custody eliminates the "company holding your money" problem
- Blockchain provides transparent, immutable transaction records
- Decentralized protocols have no "administrator" to regulate
- Internet-native businesses don't fit state-by-state models

The collision: 21st-century technology meeting 20th-century (and 19th-century) legal frameworks.
