## Operation Chokepoint 1.0: The Precedent (2013-2017)

### What It Was

**Definition:** Operation Choke Point was a U.S. Department of Justice initiative beginning in 2013 that investigated banks and pressured them to terminate relationships with legal but "high-risk" industries.

**Target industries:**
- Payday lenders
- Firearms dealers
- Online gambling
- Ammunition sales
- Adult entertainment
- Check cashing services
- Tobacco sales

### How It Worked

**The mechanism:**
1. DOJ issued subpoenas to approximately 60 financial entities
2. FDIC issued supervisory guidance labeling certain industries "high risk"
3. Bank examiners applied informal pressure during examinations
4. Banks terminated accounts to avoid regulatory friction

**The FDIC's role:**

> "DOJ began allowing two FDIC attorneys direct access to a confidential Justice Department system database named 'Operation Choke Point.' Over the next several months, FDIC attorneys utilized this database to directly participate in the program."

**The effect:**

> "Gun and ammunition dealers, payday lenders and other businesses operating legally suddenly found banks terminating their accounts with little explanation aside from 'regulatory pressure.'"

### Congressional Response

**House Oversight investigation found:**

> "Operation Choke Point was created by the DOJ to 'choke out' businesses considered 'high risk' or objectionable regardless of whether they are legal businesses, and has forced banks to terminate relationships with a wide variety of entirely lawful and legitimate merchants."

**Key criticism (Rep. Sean Duffy):**

> "I fear that activists at the DOJ and the FDIC are abusing their power and authority and are going after legal businesses and, in effect, they are weaponizing government to meet their ideological beliefs."

### Termination

**August 2017:** Trump Administration officially ended Operation Choke Point.

**FDIC settlement:**
- Promised additional training for examiners
- Agreed to cease "informal" and "unwritten suggestions" to banks
- Settled multiple lawsuits

**Sources:** [Wikipedia - Operation Choke Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Choke_Point), [House Oversight Report](https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/Staff-Report-FDIC-and-Operation-Choke-Point-12-8-2014.pdf)

## Operation Chokepoint 2.0: The Crypto Version (2021-2025)

### The Thesis

**What critics allege:**

> "Operation Chokepoint 2.0 is a term widely used by traders and crypto industry figures to refer to how financial regulators during the Biden presidential term conspired to discourage banks from doing business with crypto firms, ultimately cutting off banks from the crypto industry."

**Who named it:** Nic Carter, venture capitalist at Castle Island Ventures, was the first to identify and document the pattern.

### The Timeline

**2021: Groundwork Phase**

- OCC issued Interpretive Letter 1179
- Required banks to get "non-objection letter" before crypto custody/stablecoin activities
- This reversed Trump-era guidance that allowed banks to engage with crypto
- FDIC began probes into banks with crypto clients

**2022: Active Enforcement**

- FDIC issued "pause letters" to approximately 24 banks
- Letters instructed banks to halt or withdraw crypto services
- "Offline conversations" with bank executives
- Pattern of informal pressure without written rules

**January 3, 2023: The Joint Statement**

The Federal Reserve, FDIC, and OCC issued "Interagency Statement on Crypto-Asset Risks to Banking Organizations"

**What it said:**
- Listed "key risks" of crypto activities
- Highlighted safety and soundness concerns
- Mentioned fraud, volatility, contagion risks
- Stated agencies would take "careful and cautious approach"

**What it meant:**
- Not an outright ban
- Created regulatory uncertainty
- Sent clear signal: crypto business is risky for banks
- Exacerbated "chilling effect" on bank-crypto relationships

**March 2023: The Bank Collapses**

Three crypto-friendly banks failed in rapid succession (see below)

**2024: FOIA Revelations**

- Coinbase obtained FDIC letters through litigation
- Documents showed "pause" instructions to banks
- Evidence of coordinated approach emerged

**2025: Congressional Investigation and Reversal**

- House Financial Services Committee published damning report
- Trump administration withdrew Biden-era guidance
- Regulators formally ended restrictive policies

**Sources:** [Blockspace - OCP 2.0 Timeline](https://blockspace.media/insight/operation-chokepoint-2-0-a-complete-timeline/), [CoinDesk - Proof of OCP 2.0](https://www.coindesk.com/opinion/2024/12/06/proof-of-operation-chokepoint-2-0)

## The FDIC "Pause Letters"

### What Coinbase Found

**The FOIA battle:**
- June 2024: History Associates (hired by Coinbase) filed FOIA request
- FDIC denied request
- Lawsuit followed
- December 2024: Court ordered partial release

**What the letters revealed:**

> "According to the letters sent in 2022, the FDIC requested the institutions 'pause all crypto asset-related activity' in response to the uncertain regulations around digital assets."

**The language:**

> "We respectfully ask that you pause all crypto asset-related activity."

### The Pattern

**FDIC's approach:**
- Letters were heavily redacted
- Specific banks not identified
- Instructions varied: some stopped before starting, others told to halt expansion
- Pattern of informal pressure, not formal rules

**The timeline:**
- FDIC began issuing pause letters: March 2022 - May 2023
- Approximately 24 banks received letters
- Communications were not public until forced disclosure

### Ongoing Legal Battle (2025)

**Coinbase's allegations:**

> "The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation is deliberately blocking efforts to expose Operation Chokepoint 2.0 documents despite court orders compelling disclosure."

**Paul Grewal (Coinbase CLO):**

> "Staff at FDIC continue to stonewall our efforts to shed light on the previous administration's Operation Chokepoint 2.0."

**Judge's criticism:**
- Called FDIC's FOIA interpretation "incredibly narrow" and "illogical"
- Found FDIC "missed numerous responsive documents"
- FDIC admitted it had no litigation holds or document preservation policy

**Sources:** [CoinDesk - FDIC Letters](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2024/12/05/u-s-regulator-told-banks-to-lay-off-crypto-letters-obtained-by-coinbase-reveal), [Decrypt - FDIC Stonewalling](https://decrypt.co/333110/fdic-still-trying-to-stonewall-crypto-debanking-documents-says-coinbase-legal-chief)

## March 2023: The Bank Collapses

### The Three Banks

**Silvergate Bank:**
- Primary crypto bank
- Operated Silvergate Exchange Network (SEN) - 24/7 crypto payments
- Peak crypto deposits: $11.9 billion (Q3 2022)
- March 8, 2023: Announced voluntary liquidation

**Silicon Valley Bank (SVB):**
- Second-largest bank failure in U.S. history
- Served crypto startups and VCs
- March 10, 2023: Closed by regulators
- $42 billion in deposits fled in one day

**Signature Bank:**
- Major crypto bank
- Operated Signet - 24/7 payment platform
- $110 billion in assets
- March 12, 2023: Closed by NYDFS

### The Cascade

**Timeline:**

**March 8, 2023 (4:06 PM ET):**
- SVB issued press release announcing deposit losses and capital raise
- 30 minutes later, Silvergate announced voluntary liquidation
- VCs urged portfolio companies to pull deposits from SVB

**March 9, 2023:**
- $42 billion withdrew from SVB in single day
- Classic bank run, accelerated by social media

**March 10, 2023:**
- California DFPI closed SVB
- FDIC appointed receiver

**March 12, 2023:**
- NYDFS closed Signature Bank
- Regulators invoked systemic risk exception
- Treasury, Fed, FDIC guaranteed all depositors (not just insured)

### The Systemic Risk Exception

**What happened:**

> "After careful analysis and deliberation, the Boards of the FDIC and the Federal Reserve voted unanimously to recommend, and the Treasury Secretary, in consultation with the President, determined that the FDIC could use emergency systemic risk authorities under the Federal Deposit Insurance Act to fully protect all depositors."

**The cost:**
- SVB: $16.1 billion estimated cost to DIF
- Signature: $2.4 billion estimated cost
- Total: $18.5 billion
- Of that, $15.8 billion was for uninsured deposits

### The Crypto Angle

**Why these banks mattered:**

> "Silvergate and Signature were the two main banks for crypto companies, while Silicon Valley Bank had a lot of crypto startups and VCs as customers."

**The SEN and Signet:**
- Both offered 24/7 real-time payment platforms
- Critical infrastructure for crypto trading
- Enabled instant settlement outside banking hours
- Loss was devastating to crypto industry operations

**The debanking effect:**
- Three primary crypto banks gone in one week
- Remaining banks more cautious about crypto
- Industry scrambled to find banking alternatives

**Sources:** [CNBC - Bank Failures](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/03/12/signature-svb-silvergate-failures-effects-on-crypto-sector.html), [Bankrate - Banking Crisis Timeline](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/bank-failures-latest-updates-silicon-valley-signature-bank/)

## House Financial Services Committee Investigation

### The Report (November 2025)

**Title:** "Operation Chokepoint 2.0: Biden's Debanking of Digital Assets"

**Length:** 50 pages

**Key findings:**

1. **Fed, FDIC, and OCC** pressured banks away from crypto through informal guidance
2. **SEC** employed "enforce first, make rules never" approach
3. **At least 30 digital asset entities and individuals** lost banking access (2022-2024)
4. **FDIC letters to approximately 24 banks** documented

### The Documented Pattern

**What the Committee found:**

> "The report paints a picture of regulators publicly denying any bias against digital assets, while privately pressuring banks to sever ties with crypto firms. The evidence collected by the Committee shows a pattern of private pressure and informal coercion."

**Impact on businesses:**

> "Industry executives described account closures with as little as 24-72 hours notice. Banks terminated relationships without explanation, forcing companies to scramble to meet payroll and basic operating expenses."

### OCC Response

**December 1, 2025:** Comptroller Jonathan Gould issued statement largely agreeing with report.

**Actions taken:**
- Increased transparency in supervision
- Retired "reputation risk" as supervisory lever
- Investigating unlawful debanking by large institutions

**From OCC statement:**

> "Consistent with the President's Executive Order on Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans, the OCC continues its investigation into the role played by the largest banks in debanking digital asset customers or other legal businesses. The OCC intends to hold these banks accountable for any unlawful debanking activities it identifies."

**Sources:** [House FSC Report](https://financialservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/2025-11-30_--_fsc_debanking_report_final_1.pdf), [CoinDesk - House Report](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2025/12/01/u-s-house-lawmakers-detail-grievances-over-government-s-choke-point-2-0)

## The Reversal: Trump Administration Actions (2025)

### Regulatory Guidance Withdrawn

**March 7, 2025:** OCC withdrew from January 2023 and February 2023 joint statements

**March 28, 2025:** FDIC announced it would follow suit

**April 24, 2025:** Federal Reserve Board withdrew:
- SR 22-6 (August 2022 supervisory letter)
- SR 23-8 (August 2023 non-objection process)
- SR 23-7's Novel Activities Supervision Program
- "Reputational risk" from examination programs

### Joint Statement Withdrawal

**What was withdrawn:**

> "The withdrawn joint statements, which were issued on January 3, 2023 and February 23, 2023, addressed crypto-asset risks and liquidity risks to banking organizations resulting from crypto-asset market vulnerabilities."

**New approach:**

> "The Fed said it will no longer expect banks to provide notification of crypto or stablecoin activities and will instead monitor such activities through the normal supervisory process."

### Executive Action

**August 7, 2025:** President Trump signed executive order "Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans"

**Effect:** Formally ended Operation Chokepoint 2.0 policy approach

### New Framework

**July 2025:**
- Fed, FDIC, and OCC issued new joint statement
- Reframed risk-management considerations
- Removed "categorical discouragements"
- GENIUS Act signed (stablecoin framework)
- Presidential Working Group on Digital Asset Markets established

**Sources:** [FDIC - Agencies Withdraw Joint Statements](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2025/agencies-withdraw-joint-statements-crypto-assets), [Federal Reserve - Withdrawal Announcement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/bcreg20250424a.htm)

## Collision Points

### The Due Process Problem

**The core complaint:**

> "There's no due process. None of this is written down. There's no rules. There's no court. There's no decision process. There's no appeal."

**Why this matters:**
- Administrative guidance isn't subject to APA notice-and-comment
- Informal pressure leaves no paper trail
- Banks have discretion to terminate accounts
- No legal right to a bank account

**The structural issue:**
- Regulators can discourage activity without banning it
- Banks respond to examiner signals
- Businesses have no recourse against "soft" pressure

### The Legitimate Regulation vs. Weaponization Debate

**The government position:**
- Crypto is genuinely risky
- Banks should understand risks before engaging
- Prudential supervision requires caution
- Not targeting crypto, just managing risk

**The industry position:**
- Regulators coordinated to cut off legal industry
- Informal guidance was de facto ban
- No opportunity to demonstrate compliance
- Political motivation, not safety concerns

**The truth:**
Probably elements of both. Crypto IS risky (as FTX proved). AND regulators clearly went beyond neutral risk management.

### The "Reputation Risk" Problem

**What "reputation risk" meant in practice:**
- Examiners told banks crypto was "reputationally risky"
- No clear definition or standard
- Subjective judgment of examiners
- Became tool for discouraging disfavored activities

**Why it was removed:**
- Too vague to be fair
- Weaponizable against any disfavored industry
- Trump administration explicitly retired it from examinations

### The Coordination Question

**Evidence of coordination:**
- Joint interagency statements (documented)
- Similar pause letters to multiple banks (documented)
- San Francisco Fed 15% order (alleged in bankruptcy filing)
- Congressional report calling it "coordinated" (documented)

**What's still disputed:**
- Extent of explicit coordination vs. parallel action
- Political vs. prudential motivation
- Whether individual cases had legitimate concerns
- Full scope of the effort

### The Political Dimension

**Why crypto became political:**
- Sam Bankman-Fried donated $39.7 million to Democrats (2022)
- His fraud discredited crypto in Washington
- Republicans saw opportunity to court crypto industry
- Trump promised crypto-friendly policies
- Industry spent $238 million in 2024 elections

**The result:**
- Crypto regulation became partisan issue
- Democrats associated with Operation Chokepoint 2.0
- Republicans promised reversal
- Industry backed Trump/Republicans heavily

## Why This Matters

### The Pattern

Operation Chokepoint (both versions) demonstrates a regulatory pattern:

1. **Informal pressure** is more powerful than formal rules
2. **Bank relationships** are chokepoint for any financial activity
3. **Examination process** can discourage without explicitly banning
4. **Legal activities** can be de facto prohibited through access denial
5. **Reversal** is possible but takes political change

### For Understanding Regulation

**The lesson:**
- Reading the law isn't enough
- Regulatory "guidance" matters as much as rules
- Agency culture and priorities shape enforcement
- Access to banking is a form of permission

### For the Crypto Industry

**What changed:**
- Industry now has political voice
- Documented evidence of regulatory overreach
- New administration reversing policies
- But: Underlying bank-crypto tensions remain

**What didn't change:**
- Banks still cautious about crypto
- Examination process still subjective
- New administration can reverse again
- Need for structural legal clarity (hence GENIUS Act, etc.)

## Trajectory

### Near-Term (2025-2026)

**Regulatory reset:**
- Old guidance withdrawn
- New framework emerging
- Banks slowly re-engaging
- But caution persists

**Congressional action:**
- GENIUS Act provides stablecoin clarity
- Market structure legislation possible
- Continued oversight of past debanking

### Medium-Term Questions

**Will banks return to crypto?**
- Some will, cautiously
- Others burned, staying away
- New entrants possible with regulatory clarity

**Is it really over?**
- Administrative policies can change with administrations
- Structural legislation provides more permanence
- Bank culture changes slowly

**The fundamental question:**

How do you ensure legal businesses can access banking without regulators having to approve every activity? And how do you preserve regulators' ability to address genuine risks?

This tension isn't resolved. Operation Chokepoint 2.0 is "over" as a policy, but the underlying conflict between regulatory caution and business access remains.
