## The Enforcement Era Is Over. Now What?

Under Gary Gensler, the SEC filed 125 crypto enforcement actions and collected $6.05 billion in penalties. Georgetown Law tracked the numbers. The strategy was clear: regulate through enforcement, establish precedent through litigation.

That era ended when Paul Atkins became Chair.

In February 2025, the SEC dismissed its case against Coinbase—one of the highest-profile enforcement actions in the agency's crypto portfolio. Atkins explicitly called this the end of "regulation by enforcement."

What's replacing it? An "innovation exemption" policy expected by January 2026, no-action letters for compliant projects, and clear rules instead of retroactive investigations.

For crypto companies, this shift is more complex than it appears. Regulatory clarity isn't the same as regulatory absence.

## Understanding the Innovation Exemption

The SEC has signaled that its innovation exemption will establish clearer paths for compliant crypto projects to operate while avoiding costly traditional registrations.

This isn't deregulation. It's structured accommodation.

The December 2025 no-action letter for DTC's tokenization pilot shows what this looks like in practice. The Depository Trust Company—which settles essentially all US securities trades—received SEC staff assurance that it could operate a three-year pilot tokenizing custodied assets on supported blockchains.

The conditions were specific: tightly scoped activities, defined timeframes, ongoing reporting requirements. This is supervised innovation, not unsupervised experimentation.

For crypto projects, the innovation exemption will likely follow a similar pattern:
- Defined scope of permitted activities
- Registration or notification requirements
- Ongoing compliance obligations
- Boundaries that trigger full registration

The companies that assume innovation exemption means "anything goes" will face the same enforcement they're celebrating the end of—just under different leadership.

## The All-Republican Commission

Commissioner Caroline Crenshaw's departure left the SEC with only Republican commissioners for the first time in its modern history. This matters for policy direction, but the implications aren't what most crypto advocates assume.

The SEC's statutory mandate doesn't change with commission composition. The agency is still required to protect investors, maintain fair markets, and facilitate capital formation. Republican commissioners interpret these mandates differently than Democratic commissioners, but they don't ignore them.

After 25 years watching financial services regulation, I've observed that enforcement pendulums swing. The Gensler era's aggressive enforcement created a backlog of legal questions that courts are still resolving. The Atkins era will produce its own precedents—likely more industry-friendly, but precedents nonetheless.

The companies that build compliance infrastructure only for current enforcement priorities get caught when priorities shift. The companies that build for statutory requirements—regardless of current leadership—survive administration changes.

## What the Policy Statements Actually Say

The SEC has issued policy guidance on several crypto-specific topics:

**Mining:** Proof-of-work mining activities don't trigger securities registration if structured as computational services rather than investment contracts.

**Memecoins:** Tokens without utility or governance functions may not meet the Howey test for investment contracts—but marketing and distribution matter.

**Staking:** Staking services require careful analysis of whether they constitute securities offerings; the answer depends on program structure.

**Custody:** The Division of Trading and Markets has clarified broker-dealer physical possession requirements for crypto assets.

These aren't blanket approvals. They're frameworks for analysis. Companies that read "SEC approves staking" without reading the conditions will find themselves on the wrong side of the guidance.

## The Trap: Confusing Clarity with Permission

Here's where I've seen companies miscalculate in previous regulatory transitions: they interpret favorable regulatory signals as permission to ignore compliance.

Clear rules mean higher accountability, not lower.

When the regulatory environment was ambiguous, companies could argue reasonable interpretation. When a company faces enforcement after claiming compliance with explicit guidance, the defense is harder. "I didn't know" works in ambiguous environments. It doesn't work when the rules are published.

The SEC's shift from enforcement to guidance makes compliance more important, not less. The guidance creates a documented standard against which company conduct will be measured.

## Practical Implications for Compliance Programs

For GCs and compliance officers at crypto companies, the SEC's new approach requires specific adjustments:

**Document your analysis.** If you're relying on an SEC policy statement or no-action letter, document how your activities fit within the stated conditions. Future enforcement will examine whether reliance was reasonable.

**Monitor the boundaries.** Innovation exemptions have scope limitations. Build monitoring systems that flag when activities approach exemption boundaries. The worst position is unknowingly operating outside an exemption you thought applied.

**Prepare for registration triggers.** The exemption framework likely includes thresholds—asset sizes, user counts, transaction volumes—that trigger full registration requirements. Build the infrastructure to register before you need it.

**Watch the litigation.** The Gensler-era enforcement actions are working through courts. Those decisions will define the legal landscape regardless of SEC policy preferences. Adverse rulings don't get reversed by favorable agency guidance.

**Build for the statute, not the leadership.** Administration priorities change. Securities law doesn't. Companies that build compliance infrastructure based on statutory requirements—the actual Howey test analysis, the actual broker-dealer requirements—survive leadership transitions.

## The DTC Signal

The DTC tokenization pilot approval deserves specific attention.

DTC is the infrastructure backbone of US securities markets. When SEC staff approve DTC experimenting with blockchain settlement, that's not a niche crypto development. That's the US securities infrastructure taking blockchain seriously.

The pilot conditions—three years, tightly scoped, specific blockchains—tell you what the SEC considers acceptable innovation parameters. These aren't arbitrary restrictions; they're the template for how the innovation exemption framework will likely work.

For crypto companies building securities-adjacent products, the DTC pilot conditions are the clearest signal of what "compliant innovation" looks like to this SEC.

## Building for the New Era

The SEC's policy shift creates opportunity for companies that approach it correctly:

**Opportunity 1:** Products that were enforcement targets under Gensler may find pathways to compliant operation. Evaluate whether your offerings fit within the emerging guidance.

**Opportunity 2:** The innovation exemption framework may reduce registration costs for qualifying projects. Monitor the January 2026 guidance for eligibility requirements.

**Opportunity 3:** Institutional partners who avoided crypto due to SEC enforcement risk may reconsider. The compliance infrastructure you built during the enforcement era becomes a competitive advantage.

**Risk 1:** Competitors who cut compliance corners during the enforcement era may undercut on cost. Resist the temptation to match their approach—the next enforcement cycle will distinguish between them.

**Risk 2:** The policy framework is not yet final. Companies that build based on anticipated rules may need to adjust when actual rules differ.

The enforcement era is over. The compliance era continues.
